Headline. The High Governance portfolio carries materially and significantly lower risk (max drawdown, volatility, downside deviation; all p<0.0001, large effect sizes) — but this did not translate into better performance: annualized returns and Sharpe ratios are statistically indistinguishable between the groups. Governance behaves as a risk-reduction signal, not a performance-enhancement one, and cannot be said to prove anything causal about future returns.

1 · Study design

Universe Top 10% (High Governance) and bottom 10% (Low Governance) of securities by Governance Pillar Score from the GridExport file. After dropping private names (e.g., SpaceX) and any without price history, 112 vs. 112 tickers remained.

Prices Historical adjusted close (dividend/split-adjusted), 1,254 trading days, 19 Jul 2021 – 16 Jul 2026. Daily returns used directly; monthly from month-end resampling.

Metrics Per ticker: Max Drawdown (peak-to-trough %), Annualized Volatility (σdaily×√252; monthly ×√12), Downside Deviation (σ of negative returns, annualized).

Test Welch's t-test (unequal variance) on group means. Two-sided p reported; all one-sided tests in the "lower risk" direction are also significant.

4 · Interpretation

Better governance is associated with calmer, more resilient equity behavior. High Governance names swung roughly half as much (33.5% vs 58.4% annualized volatility) and, critically, their worst peak-to-trough loss averaged −45.7% versus −61.2% for Low Governance — a ~15-point cushion in the most painful scenario. Downside deviation, which isolates losing days, tells the same story (23.1% vs 37.1%).

The monthly results confirm the daily ones, so the effect is not an artifact of short-term noise. Large effect sizes (|d| ≈ 0.7–1.1) mean the gap is not just statistically detectable but economically meaningful.

5 · Performance & risk-adjusted return (Sharpe)

Lower risk is only half the question — the decisive test is whether High Governance also delivered better performance. It did not. Raw returns are statistically indistinguishable, and on a risk-adjusted basis (Sharpe, rf = 3%) the two groups are effectively tied.

Key insight from Sharpe. High Governance cut volatility roughly in half — but its raw returns were also lower (12.4% vs 15.8%). The two effects cancel: Sharpe is ~0.29 vs ~0.25, a difference that is not statistically significant (p = 0.58). So better governance bought lower risk, not better risk-adjusted performance.

7 · Does this prove governance reduces future risk and improves performance?

No — and it is important to be precise about what the data can and cannot support:

  • Reduces risk? Strongly supported as an association. Lower volatility, drawdown, and downside deviation are all highly significant (p < 0.0001) with large effect sizes. But this is one 5-year window measured cross-sectionally — it cannot prove causation, and "future" risk is an extrapolation, not a demonstrated fact.
  • Improves performance? Not supported. Neither raw returns (p = 0.33) nor Sharpe ratios (p = 0.58) differ significantly. High Governance was calmer but not more rewarding on a risk-adjusted basis.
  • Why "prove" is the wrong word. Proving causation about the future would require: (1) a forward-looking, out-of-sample test (rank today, measure realised returns later); (2) controls for confounders — sector, size, beta, and age, since Low-Gov names skew toward speculative recent IPOs; and (3) replication across multiple market regimes, not just the 2021–2026 window that includes the 2022 sell-off.
Bottom line. Higher governance is reliably associated with lower risk, shows no reliable link to higher returns or Sharpe, and has no meaningful relationship with price level. The evidence supports governance as a risk-reduction signal, not a performance-enhancement or causal-forecasting one.

8 · Controlled test — does the effect survive confounders?

A key objection: Low-Governance names skew small, high-beta, and speculative, so the risk gap might just be those traits. To check, each forward outcome was regressed on governance plus controls (log market cap, point-in-time beta where relevant, and GICS sector fixed effects). 9 · Forward out-of-sample test — design & how to run it

Everything above is cross-sectional (one window). To test whether governance genuinely predicts the future, the script forward_test.py implements a true out-of-sample design:

  • Rank at a past formation date (default 2021-07-15) using governance known at that time, then measure the risk/return each name subsequently realised through 2026.
  • Point-in-time beta estimated from a pre-formation window (no look-ahead), plus company age (years listed at formation, from first-trade date) and log size & sector as controls.
  • Outputs both a top-vs-bottom-decile Welch test on forward outcomes and a controlled regression of forward risk/return/Sharpe on governance.
One data requirement to make it genuinely predictive. The test must rank on governance as it stood in 2021, but the GridExport only holds current (2026) scores. Feeding current scores in creates look-ahead bias — the script runs but prints a loud warning and the result is contemporaneous, not forward. To run it properly, supply governance_pit.csv (columns: RIC, gov) from a GridExport pulled with the as-of date set to mid-2021. Then: python forward_test.py.

10 · Caveats

  • Association, not proof of causation. Low-governance names here skew toward speculative, recent-IPO, and high-beta profiles; governance may be a marker for that risk profile rather than its sole cause.
  • Survivorship & window. The 2021–2026 window includes the 2022 drawdown, which amplifies differences between stable and speculative names. Results can shift in other regimes.
  • Data source. Prices are yfinance adjusted close, which can differ marginally from LSEG total-return series; magnitudes may move slightly but the ranking and significance are robust.
  • Risk ≠ return. This analysis measures risk only. Lower risk does not by itself imply higher future returns.
